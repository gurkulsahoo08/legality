While the recent controversy surrounding Medha Roopam Case, largely captured media attention towards familial connections of Justice SC Sharma. There is another side which hasn’t gotten as much attention truly required compared to the former raised point, that is “Master of Roster”. This problem is uniquely visible in India out of all democracies who follow the system of dividing their Supreme or Constitutional courts into panel like Germany. But many Supreme or Constitutional Courts around the world to avoid the problem of “Master of Roster” sit en banc, like SCOTUS. It is also one of the points that I believe needs immediate reforms to clamp down arbitrariness in decision making in SC, the Master of Roster gives CJI near dictatorial or at worst unusually concentrated power to single-handedly decide outcome of cases, assuming of course he knows the biases of every Judge and Judge doesn’t get swayed and produces a ruling which doesn’t align with his previous patterns. I am today going to start with history of how CJI gained such power, before that who decided bench composition, abuse of this power and finally solutions from existing system in other democratic countries and a conclusion. Also I would be later putting a master list article outlining which reforms are urgently needed in Supreme Court, and as I write in detail in future, I am going to hyperlink from that list.

History#

Before Constitution#

Well to find out where the master of roster power of CJI originated, I had to go through a lot of academic papers, most of them were paywalled, I also found a paper written by Amit Kumar on ResearchGate1 and another wonderful paper written by A Kundu, U Jhunjhunwala in NLUJ law review.2 So in England there was a court called Court of Chancery in medieval times it originated in 14th and 15th centuries as an administrative and judicial body headed by the Lord Chancellor3 and existed till 1875, upon which it was merged into the newly formed High Court by Judicature Acts of 1873–1875.4 In this court there was a clerical position known as Master of the Rolls.12 Later an entire department was established under Master of the Rolls.12 In 17th century, gradually over time Court of Chancery became independent of King and due to this transformation Master of the Rolls got huge powers and was entrusted with the ultimate power of deciding the court’s roster and other administrative duties.12 Now England abolished this system quickly after realising that the power had become centralised in one position or person, Now Master of the Rolls is just the President of the Civil Division of the Court of Appeal of England and Wales and Head of Civil Justice and second in seniority in England and Wales only to the Lord Chief Justice.12 I nearly believed this claim after all it was published in peer reviewed NLUJ law review journal, but it is in fact wrong, I found no indications upon reading the cited sources by the paper that said Master of the Rolls controlled seal of the king, or was his secretary or even controlled roster, Also roster in modern sense wouldn’t have existed back then. Also upon looking at cited sources I came to the realization that NLUJ law review authors may have confused two roles, The role of lord chancellor and master of the rolls who was a clerk under Lord Chancellor and Lord Chancellor controlled the docket of Court of Chancery as Court of Chancery was effectively a one judge court till 19th century presided over solely by Lord Chancellor.56

Infact colonial administrators in India created the position of master of roster as understood in modern sense, Section 200 of GOI Act, 1935 established the Federal Court and Section 214(3) explicitly inserted “Subject to the provisions of any rules of court, the Chief Justice of India shall determine what judges are to constitute any division of the court and what judges are to sit for any purpose.” 1

On June 6, 1949, Dr Bhimrao Ambedkar would go on record in Constitutional assembly debates and say “I may also point out that the provisions contained in article 121 are the same as the provisions contained in article 214 of the Government of India Act, 1935 relating to the Federal Court and article 224 relating to the High Courts. Therefore, there is really no departure from the position as it exists today.” 7 Another member on the same day in CAD, Bakshi Tek Chand will say “Now, Sir, these all are matters which ought to be solely within the jurisdiction of the Chief Justice and the judges of the Supreme Court and there is no reason why they should be subject to approval of the President. If you see the constitution of the High Courts, as they have functioned in the country for the last eighty years or more and also the provisions of the Government of India Acts of 1915 and 1935 relating to these matters, you will find that it is purely within the jurisdiction of the Chief Justice and the judges of the High Court to frame rules in such matters, as the admission of advocates, attorneys, etc. and the constitution of Benches.” 8 This clearly shows that Constituent Assembly believed that administrative power and master of roster will lie with the Chief Justice of the court.

After Constitution#

Till now it had not become a severe problem because Federal Court in colonial times used to sit en banc in 1940s and 1930s (an assumption because Federal court had 3 judges and did not divide itself into panels) but after subsequent increase in number of judges in 1950s starting from 8 judges SC adopted panel system of two or three judges in a single panel, it became difficult to sit en banc.1910 For 1950s I couldn’t find any accurate source but it can be safely assumed that CJI was master of roster by convention because looking at composition of bench and its biases and the fact that the Chief Justice has been about 6.5 times less likely to be in dissent than other judges on constitution benches since independence.9 K. Subba Rao, he dissented 48 times as a judge, more than any other judge up to that point, but never after becoming Chief Justice. During his tenure the Court as a whole gave more anti-government decisions than at any earlier point, because most likely K. Subba Rao formed benches composed of like minded Judge.911 So it can be presumed from this that in 1950s CJI was indeed master of roster.9 But it was well established by law that Chief Justice of High courts were the master of the roster through several constitutional cases and orders dating back from 1949 till 1990s, I have listed a few:

  1. Rajasthan High Court Ordinance, 1949, para 44, and the 1952 High Court Rules (Rules 54, 55, 73)
  2. State v. Devi Dayal, AIR 1959 Allahabad 421
  3. Niranjan Singh v. State, AIR 1974 Rajasthan 171
  4. State of Maharashtra v. Narayan Shamrao Puranik, AIR 1982 SC 1198
  5. Mayavaram Financial Corporation v. Registrar of Chits (1991), Madras HC
  6. Inder Mani v. Matheshwari Prasad, (1996) 6 SCC 587
  7. Sohan Lal Baid v. State of West Bengal, AIR 1990 Calcutta 168

but again all these relate to Chief Justices of High Courts only. Also I took a look at Supreme Court Rules, 1966 and found out these lines from Order VII

“Subject to the other provisions of these rules every cause, appeal or matter shall be heard by a Bench consisting of not less than two Judges nominated by the Chief Justice” 12

This tells us, despite constitutional silence and no statutory law or judicial precedent, Chief Justice was Master of Roster, since at least 1966, according to rules of SC.12

State Of Rajasthan vs Prakash Chand & Ors#

In 1998 we get our first defining judgement, State Of Rajasthan vs Prakash Chand & Ors. Now I will divide the State Of Rajasthan vs Prakash Chand & Ors into facts and holdings sub section to make readers understand easily. We need to remember this specific judgement because it would become very important later on in establishing judicially that CJI is indeed the master of roster and formalising the convention that has continued since pre independence era.

Facts#

  1. A PIL in the Rajasthan High Court at Jodhpur (W.P. 2949/1996) sought accommodation and benefits for the High Court judges. An intervenor also challenged the constitution of the High Court’s Jaipur bench. Justice B.J. Shethna heard it as a single judge.13

  2. The roster changed, so Shethna J. was required to sit in a Division Bench from 4 September 1997. The State then applied to refer the petition to a Division Bench because it raised constitutional questions.13

  3. Chief Justice Mukul Gopal Mukherjee sent it to a Division Bench, which dismissed it as infructuous on 10 September.13

  4. Meanwhile Shethna J. kept trying to list “part-heard” cases before himself on a separate board. The Chief Justice’s order of 3 September said that providing the roster is the Chief Justice’s prerogative.13

  5. While hearing an unrelated criminal revision (Prakash Chand’s, a conviction under Section 304A IPC), Shethna J. called for the disposed-of writ petition’s record. He made scathing comments about the Chief Justice and the Division Bench, and issued a show-cause notice for criminal contempt against the Chief Justice. He also accused former Chief Justices, including CJI J.S. Verma, of illegally drawing daily allowance and misappropriating public funds.13

Holding#

The Supreme Court allowed the State’s appeal. It quashed the contempt notice and expunged all of Shethna J.’s comments, held that he had no authority to call for the record, and found his remarks on daily allowance to be factually and legally wrong. It also listed 14 conclusions, including that the Chief Justice is master of the roster, that puisne judges cannot pick and choose cases, and that the Chief Justice can refer even a part-heard case to a larger bench.13

Supreme Court Rules, 2013#

Now Supreme Court Rules, 2013 were officially published in Gazette of India on May 29, 2014.14 The Order VI explicitly says,

“Subject to the other provisions of these rules every cause, appeal or matter shall be heard by a Bench consisting of not less than two Judges nominated by the Chief Justice” 14

“Where in the course of the hearing of any cause, appeal or other proceeding, the Bench considers that the matter should be dealt with by a larger Bench, it shall refer the matter to the Chief Justice, who shall thereupon constitute such a Bench for the hearing of it.” 14

Now this will lead to first CJAR (2018) and then CJAR would lead to Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India.

Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India#

Facts#

Senior advocate Shanti Bhushan, a former Law Minister, filed under Article 32, represented by Dushyant Dave with Prashant Bhushan. He asked the Court to read “Chief Justice of India” in the Supreme Court Rules, 2013 as a collegium of the five senior-most judges. He also asked that the Registry consult senior judges when listing, that listing follow the Rules and not “picking and choosing” benches, and that on urgent mentions only a date be fixed, with the bench set by the Rules. His concern was that the CJI could assign sensitive cases to chosen judges to get a predetermined outcome. Attorney General K.K. Venugopal replied that daily bench allocation by a collegium would be unworkable and cause chaos.15

Holding#

  1. Sikri J. said the position is undisputed and traced it through Prakash Chand (1998), the Constitution Bench in Campaign for Judicial Accountability and Reforms (2018), which applied Prakash Chand to the Supreme Court, and the three-judge decision in Asok Pande (11 April 2018).15

  2. He accepted that the Constitution is silent on the point and that the position rests on convention that is now engrafted in the Rules. He cited Section 214(3) of the 1935 Act and Order VI Rule 1 of the 2013 Rules, which have the CJI nominate the judges for every bench.15

  3. The Judges’ cases were tied to Article 124 and judicial appointments, so their logic did not extend to roster work, which is daily and administrative.15

  4. The Court said the CJI should act by consultation, with regard to judges’ abilities and specialisation, and that listing must follow the 2013 Rules and the Handbook.15

Campaign for Judicial Accountability and Reforms (2018)#

As I told earlier to keep State of Rajasthan v. Prakash Chand in mind, as it will play a large role here.

Facts#

  1. In September 2017, the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) registered an FIR (RC. 10(A)/2017-AC.III) against individuals including I.M. Quddusi (a retired Odisha High Court judge) and representatives of the Prasad Education Trust. The FIR alleged a criminal conspiracy to use illegal gratification to secure favourable judicial orders for de-recognised medical colleges. The underlying medical college matter had previously been heard by a Supreme Court bench headed by Chief Justice Dipak Misra.16

  2. CJAR filed a writ petition under Article 32 of the Constitution alleging high-level judicial corruption and seeking an independent Special Investigation Team (SIT) headed by a retired CJI to investigate the matter. A nearly identical petition was filed by advocate Kamini Jaiswal (Kamini Jaiswal v. Union of India, W.P. (Crl.) No. 176 of 2017).16

  3. On November 8, 2017, CJAR mentioned its petition before Court No. 2, presided over by Justice J. Chelameswar (the second senior-most judge) and Justice S. Abdul Nazeer, who issued notice and listed it for November 10.16

  4. On November 9, 2017, the companion petition by Kamini Jaiswal was mentioned before the same two-judge bench (Chelameswar and Nazeer, JJ.), which passed an order directing that the matter be placed before a Constitution Bench comprising the five senior-most judges of the Supreme Court.16

  5. On the morning of November 10, 2017, CJAR’s petition was listed before a two-judge bench of Justices A.K. Sikri and Ashok Bhushan. Noting the November 9 order, that bench directed the matter to be placed before the Chief Justice of India for appropriate orders regarding listing.16

  6. On the afternoon of November 10, 2017, CJI Dipak Misra constituted a five-judge Constitution Bench (which did not include Justice Chelameswar or the next three senior-most collegium judges).16

Holding#

  1. The Constitution Bench held that the Chief Justice of India alone holds the administrative authority to constitute benches and assign judicial rosters. The Court extended the principles in State of Rajasthan v. Prakash Chand (1998) 1 SCC 1 from High Court Chief Justices directly to the Supreme Court.16

  2. On the judicial side, the CJI is equal to every other judge of the Supreme Court. However, on the administrative side, the CJI possesses singular primacy over the Court’s roster.16

  3. No two-judge or three-judge bench has the jurisdiction to allocate matters to themselves, mandate bench compositions, or direct the CJI to list a case before a specific panel.16

  4. The Court held that any order by another bench directing the listing of a matter before a specific set of judges was non est, ineffective in law, and not binding on the CJI. Bypassing the CJI’s roster authority was held to risk “utter confusion” and institutional chaos.16

Abuses#

Four Judges’ Press Conference#

This was the most prolific case of CJI abusing his master of roster power that led to a huge fallout and first of its kind in Indian legal history. On January 12, 2018, the four senior-most judges of the Supreme Court: Justices J. Chelameswar, Ranjan Gogoi, Madan B. Lokur, and Kurian Joseph, held an unprecedented public press conference at Chelameswar’s residence.17

The immediate trigger was the allocation of public interest litigation (PIL) petitions seeking an independent investigation into the mysterious death of Special CBI Judge B.H. Loya (who was presiding over the Sohrabuddin Sheikh encounter case). Despite being an exceptionally sensitive matter, it was assigned to Court 10, headed by a relatively junior judge, Justice Arun Mishra.17

We all know how this event went, CJI Misra would exclude all four judges who spoke against him from the seven important cases to be heard by constitution benches in coming week as decided on roster on January 16.18

Later Asok Pande would bring a petition which stated “unfettered power was being exercised by the Chief Justices in the matter of formation of benches” and called for reforms in master of roster system. A bench consisting of Misra, Khanwilkar and Chandrachud rejected the petition on 11 April. Later Chandrachud will write “the Chief Justice of India is first among equals and has the authority to decide allocation of cases and setting up of benches.” 19

The abuse could be seen with naked eye from a mile away, and this manifestly arbitrary abuse led to first demands for reforms albeit suppressed by the very same Court and Judge it was advocating against. In an ideal system Dipak Misra should have recused from the case regarding deciding power of roster but he did not do so, that again points to another weak point in our judicial system that is there exists no mandatory recusal policy unlike in other countries who have strict statutory guidelines for recusal and potentially annulment of judgement on appeal if conflict of interest is discovered like US.

Other abuses in 2023#

In late 2023, several senior members of the Supreme Court Bar Association, including Senior Advocates Dushyant Dave and Prashant Bhushan, submitted formal open letters to CJI D.Y. Chandrachud raising alarms over systemic listing irregularities. Numerous politically charged matters such as bail pleas of activists booked under UAPA (including Umar Khalid and Bhima Koregaon accused) and petitions by opposition political figures were repeatedly shifted away from benches that had already heard substantive arguments. Instead, they were reassigned to a specific bench headed by a junior judge (Justice Bela M. Trivedi), in direct contravention of the Supreme Court’s published listing conventions, which prescribe that part heard matters remain before the senior judge. Then out of the blue, a long-pending contempt plea concerning the central government’s delay in notifying judicial appointments was suddenly deleted from the cause list of Court 2 (presided over by the second-senior-most judge, Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul). To which Justice Kaul directly replied on record “I am sure that the Chief Justice must be aware of it… I have not deleted the matter or expressed unwillingness to take it. Some things are better left unsaid.” 2021

Medha Roopam Case#

Now this directly brings us back to 2026, to the Medha Roopam case. Now we need to first understand the fact that who is Medha Roopam and what she did. Medha Roopam is a UP cadre IAS officer, whose father is current CEC Gyanesh Kumar (in hot waters for arguably more severe matter). Now Medha Roopam invoked NSA against Delhi University student activist Akriti Chaudhary following a labor protest in Noida (labor protest itself occurred in April 2026 and Akriti herself had been arrested earlier on protest site.) on the recommendation of police in May 2026.22 On September 2, 2026 a division Bench comprising Justice Atul Sreedharan and Justice Achal Sachdev quashed the detention order after nearly five months of custody on the fact that DM acted without application of mind to “make an example” and court awarded ₹5 lakh in compensation to Chaudhary, ordering the sum to be recovered from the personal salaries of Medha Roopam and other responsible police personnel, alongside adverse entries in their service books.22 But Medha Roopam and her lawyer immediately appealed to SC and on Sept 23, 2026 a Supreme Court bench presided over by Justice Satish Chandra Sharma and Justice N. Kotiswar Singh stayed the operation of the High Court’s directions concerning the ₹5 lakh salary deduction and judicial strictures against the officers pending regular hearing.23 It was later clarified that the High Court’s quashing of the NSA detention was not stayed by the interim order.24

Now this was the basic timeline of events, but a concern arises and first raised was that Justice SC Sharma’s son is in ECI standing counsel as an advocate in Madhya Pradesh HC and his other son is also in MP government panel as an advocate.25 Now again I talked about this in last article regarding the two judge bench decision on ECI Act of Parliament and how it got referred to a higher bench and how personalities like Saurav Das and Prashant Bhushan immediately began accusing Justice SC sharma of being biased and spreading rumor that he has been guaranteed a post retirement seat by Government.26 I discussed it at length to defend the decision (it was constitutional) and point out the problems with the argument presented and even took a deep dive into history where such condition existed but the government still lost.26 But I mentioned in that article that I would write separately about Medha Roopam case as it was irregular.26 Now again I would not go onto Justice SC Sharma familial connections as those points as I said in previous article may appear significant but are worthless except the recusal and disclosure one.26

What I want to focus on is the role of CJI Suryakant and how he subverted the roster and gave the case to junior judges instead of the senior one who should have heard it in the first place. This is a classic textbook case of Judicial deference in face of a roaring executive. If we go and access the official roster list of SC, we would find that matters related to habeas corpus and preventive detention have been assigned to CJI Suryakant, Justice Vikram Nath, Justice BV Nagarathna and Justice MM Sundresh only.27 Also not to mention the fact that the accused in this case Akriti, had earlier approached SC and her case was placed before the benches of Justice BV Nagarathna and Justice Ujjal Bhuyan.28 We could also further see that all other habeas corpus cases got correctly listed before the above mentioned first four benches only.28 Only Medha Roopam case got handed to the bench led by Justice SC Sharma.28 Now the roster also says Justice SC Sharma could take on other matters if specifically assigned but for that special matter can only be assigned by CJI who is the master of roster.28 Also not to mention as pointed out during the hearing, after briefly hearing Solicitor General and senior counsel for the State, the Bench muted the audio of the virtual proceedings and closed media access to the video feed for the remainder of the hearing.29 Again this fueled a lot of early speculation about the case and certainly damaged reputation of court.

Now again usually connected matters are listed before the same benches, so we could have logically said that Akriti matter was already once heard by Justice BV Nagarathna bench (even though it was disposed by May 8 with liberty to approach HC)28 and should have been listed before her bench again also other Noida-protest matters are pending before Justice BV Nagarathna,2829 but CJI curiously referred it to a much junior bench and already knowing that the Judge had familial connections with ECI. Also a point to consider from the other side that this is a mere interim order and final outcome has not been pronounced, so we couldn’t say that it is all rigged, maybe the interim pause was only for the judges to dig deeper into the matter. I would say on this point that social media speculators have overblown the familal connection issue over a wide range while ignoring the fact that this is not a final order and merely speculating or using supports of rumors saying that Justice SC Sharma is biased. This is plainly browbeating a judge because he didn’t issue an order that the social media speculators wanted. I have spoken at lenght about this type of browbeating in my previous article.26

Even if we concede the above point to opposition that this is merely an interim order, we still arrive at a case of abuse of roster. It can be probably derived that CJI Kant didn’t want to upset the executive over this matter and chose a path of deference. If we look at the past cases of the aforementioned benches listed for habeas corpus petition, we find that their orders are usually pro rights or pro people, not a shocking discovery. Also we know from past case history that justice BV Nagarathna usually doesn’t shy away from ruling against government in personal liberty matters and Also we have no reason given for why the matter was assigned to this particular bench, this is another huge problem of SC that is institutional opacity which it uses to frame judicial independence, The entire structure of collegium is rested on the fact that Judges selecting Judges through a Institutionally opaque system would somehow guarantee institutional independence. Also opacity is practiced by SC because somehow common people knowing the reason why such a administrative measure was taken would somehow lower institutional independence.

Reforms and Conclusion#

As I have said, Indian SC is not unique in the aspect of dividing the courts into panel, many other courts also sit panel wise like Supreme Court of UK or Constitutional Court in Germany or ECHR. While many solutions exist, the most favorable I find is that followed by ECHR, I have read ECHR rules of court and German Constitutional Court rules303132 and interpolated it to the Indian context and drew up an abstract of how such reform will look like.

First is an annual allocation plan, and the full court must adopt it unanimously at the starting of each year. The plan will lay down subject categories, standing panels, the rotation schedule and an urgent-matters protocol, and then it would be published to the public. Now second thing we go to is standing panels, the court is divided into panels for a fixed term, Composition is based on Seniority, Origin and Gender as decided in annual allocation plan and adopted by full court unanimously. Third the cases go by subject category, then are assigned among panels by round-robin or random draw, with an audit log this significantly reduces power of CJI under current master of roster system as the cases now get assigned randomly on probability. Fourth each bench is drawn from its panel by a published rotation, with the senior-most judge presiding. Part-heard matters stay with their bench. Fifth, within a bench, the lead judge is chosen under principles set for the year as laid down in annual allocation plan. Disagreements go to the panel. Sixth the most comprehensive one, which lays down how constitutional benches are formed, referral decisions go to a rotating referral panel with six-month terms, not a single bench. Under Article 145(3), at least five judges are needed. The CJI and the next most senior judge sit ex officio, and the remainder are drawn by lot, with balance criteria. The draw is public, with the Registrar present and minutes published. Seventh a binding recusal rule is published and in cases of non disclosure the decision is automatically annulled. Eighth is the oversight part, I am borrowing this one from German Basic Law, which speaks of “lawful judges”. In this system if any deviation occurs and one of the sides thinks the deviation is not correct or believes it to be malafide then the party can flag a breach and try to reverse the decision by arguing that the case was not heard by a “lawful judge”. Also an annual public report on deviations and a route for litigants to flag a breach.

The SC should separately also address their opaque system, while my proposed reforms make many things public, the other parts of SC like the appointment procedure by collegium is too opaque and it must be replaced, I will write about it in detail, some other time. Arguably SC using opacity to defend institutional independence causes more harm to its reputation and standing than it would to its independence if it was an open book. Also other supreme courts like Constitutional Court in Germany or ECHR or Constitutional court in South Africa have stellar judicial reputation and arguably are more shielded from interference and enjoy more judicial independence because they are not opaque, so when a judgement is pronounced nobody can point finger toward the institution and say “it was biased” or “procedure was violated”, unlike in Medha Roopam case where institutional opacity immediately led to finger pointing by Social Media and fueling fire to the wider allegations of conspiracy that were already growing around independent institutions like SC or ECI. I would like to point out that Nepal has also abolished the master of roster system.33 But India being the world’s largest democracy which would arguably requires a more robust and vigilant Judiciary, instead got one filled with opacity. This has led to recent and huge wave of loss of public trust crisis. It is high time that SC does reforms to bring transparency as it cannot afford to lose constitutional legitimacy as an independent court in front of the public eyes in a country where independent institutions are increasingly viewed with mistrust.
